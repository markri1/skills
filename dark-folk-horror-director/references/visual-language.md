# Visual Language

Use this reference to make settings, people, entities, and cinematography belong to the same dark folk-horror universe without making every result look alike.

## Creative identity

The world sits somewhere between medieval folklore and the late 19th century: dark historical folk horror, ancient superstition, rural isolation, decaying civilization, and nature reclaiming human places. Useful traditions include Slavic, Nordic, Baltic, Central and Eastern European, Appalachian, frontier, bayou, and Southern Gothic folklore.

Aim for some combination of dread, loneliness, mystery, decay, melancholy, ritual, forbidden knowledge, vulnerability, and ancient evil. Horror should usually feel old. The emotional premise is that something existed here long before the viewer arrived, and the world is indifferent to human survival.

Avoid generic modern horror, pristine fantasy, clean Hollywood polish, excessive jump scares, and spectacle that explains away the mystery.

## Period and physical world

Default to roughly 1200–1890 when the user does not specify a period. Exact scholarship is optional unless requested, but tools, clothing, architecture, travel, light sources, and materials must feel physically plausible for a pre-modern or early industrial setting. Do not introduce obvious modern objects without a reason.

Favor weathered, handmade, used materials:

- aged or blackened timber, mud, stone, moss, wet vegetation
- rusty iron, old rope, primitive tools, bone
- worn linen, wool, leather, faded cloth
- candles, wax, dried herbs, handmade devotional or ritual objects

Surfaces should carry age, moisture, soot, dirt, repair, and human use.

## Environmental range

Do not default to a forest. Rotate environments and let the setting participate in the threat.

- Eastern European: wooden villages, Orthodox chapels, marsh settlements, burial mounds, birch woods, frozen roads, monasteries, mountain hamlets, pagan clearings
- Nordic and Baltic: dark pine woods, frozen lakes, fjords, turf-roof cottages, fishing villages, snowy graveyards, misty coasts, isolated cabins
- Western and Central European: medieval villages, ruined monasteries, wind-beaten farmland, moorland, plague settlements, mills, forest chapels, stone farmhouses, pilgrimage roads
- American folk horror: Louisiana bayous, flooded cemeteries, ruined plantations, decaying churches, frontier towns, Appalachian woods, river settlements, muddy Reconstruction-era roads, burned homesteads, abandoned mines

Change the weather and season as well: winter haze, rain, flooded ground, damp summer heat, distant storms, hard frost, dawn fog, and wind can each create a different form of isolation.

## Light and color

Use restrained natural or period light: moonlight, overcast daylight, weak winter sun, candles, lanterns, fire, dawn fog, dusk, distant lightning, or light filtering through trees. Favor underexposure, deep shadow, low visibility, atmospheric haze, silhouettes, and obscured backgrounds. Darkness should hide information.

Base colors on muddy brown, charcoal, faded black, cold grey, desaturated green, moss, dirty beige, weathered wood, faded burgundy, dried-blood red, pale moonlight, and amber firelight. Restrained accents may include religious gold, faded blue, ritual red thread, white bone, or candle yellow.

Avoid neon color, glossy fantasy illumination, highly saturated grading, routine blue-and-orange contrast, and perfectly illuminated subjects.

## Camera and image character

The camera should feel physically present and potentially unsafe. Choose the approach that serves the scene:

- first-person or shoulder-height observation
- cautious handheld movement or slow tracking
- peeking around a tree, doorway, or structure
- following something at a distance
- a static frame that allows a disturbing detail to emerge
- a restrained push-in or slow reveal
- subtle shake from breathing, footing, or fatigue
- cinematic third-person framing when it better serves the idea

Do not force first-person perspective. Avoid routine drone shots, hyperactive movement, impossible paths, constant rapid cuts, or video-game action framing unless requested.

Aim for cinematic realism with documentary imperfection: slight underexposure, subtle grain, natural lens flaws, selective shallow depth of field, drifting fog or smoke, moisture, particles, imperfect focus, and motion blur caused by actual movement. Use only the qualities that are visible and relevant.

## People

Ground characters in labor, place, and vulnerability. Possible roles include hunter, traveler, farmer, widow, monk, priest, herbalist, elder, gravedigger, fisherman, woodsman, soldier, outlaw, pilgrim, witch, doctor, trapper, occultist, or bounty hunter.

Make them tired, dirty, weather-beaten, wary, physically vulnerable, and plausibly dressed. Avoid glamorous fantasy heroes unless the user explicitly wants one.

## Entities

Connect supernatural beings to local ecology, labor, belief, or historical trauma. Sources may include Baba Yaga, Leshy, Rusalka, Strigoi, Draugr, trolls, witches, revenants, forest spirits, plague figures, pagan gods, cursed hunters, shapeshifters, swamp entities, forgotten saints, ritual groups, or invented folklore.

Use famous entities sparingly. Strong original beings often combine familiar human qualities with one wrong detail: a small anatomical distortion, ritual clothing, worn natural materials, an obscured face, an ambiguous silhouette, or movement that violates expectation. Reveal less than the full design when possible.

Avoid default zombies, giant-horned demons, glossy CGI monsters, oversized teeth, and glowing eyes unless the user asks for them.

## Folk knowledge

When it adds value, show that local people know a rule the outsider does not. Express it through behavior or material evidence rather than exposition: shutters closed before sunset, a repeated mark above every door, offerings left outside, an avoided road, dead animals oriented deliberately, a forbidden name, missing mirrors, or a taboo around a bridge, bell, song, or whistle.

Invent rules suited to the exact culture and setting. Do not force this device into an isolated shot where it distracts from the brief.

## Variation check

Across concepts, rotate country, folklore, century, landscape, occupation, entity, weather, perspective, story structure, and degree of supernatural certainty. Do not repeatedly fall back on witches, Baba Yaga, forests, hooded cultists, antlered monsters, or glowing eyes. Consistency should come from mood, material reality, and camera language—not recycled nouns.
